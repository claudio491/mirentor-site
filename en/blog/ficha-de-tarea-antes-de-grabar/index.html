The one-page sheet you need before recording a task

How to describe a manual task on one page: where it starts, what enters the frame, what varies and who knows it. It helps you decide whether it is worth recording.

Almost every business has a task that "everyone knows how to do" and nobody has written down in full. Before you think about cameras, you should be able to describe it on one page. If you cannot, you are not ready to record it yet, and that is a useful answer too.

This guide proposes a one-page sheet for describing a manual task. It works for your first conversation with Mirentor, and it also helps you understand your own operation. It is not an official program form and does not guarantee that a task will be accepted; conditions are confirmed case by case.

Write where it starts and where it ends

A task has a first movement and a last one. "Packing orders" is too broad. "Take an order from the shelf, check it against the list, put it in a box and close it" is something you can picture.

Write those two points in your own words. Then count the steps in between. If there are more than eight or ten, it is probably two tasks, and it is clearer to describe them separately.

Note what enters the frame

A camera worn at eye level captures hands, objects and part of the surroundings. Walk through the task in your head and list what would appear: tools, products, tables, shelves and, above all, what should not appear.

Check for screens with customer data, labels with names, documents on the table and people walking behind. For each one, decide whether it can be moved, covered or excluded. This step connects to what we covered in the guide on personal data: what shows in the frame can be other people's information.

Count how much it changes from one time to the next

Two questions help. Are the objects always the same, or do they change in size, weight or shape? Is the space the same, or does it get rearranged during the day? A task with a lot of variation is harder to describe, but it can also be more interesting to evaluate.

Do not make up numbers here. If you do not know how many times the task repeats in a shift, say so and ask the person who does it. An honest answer is worth more than a round number.

Mark what is uncomfortable or slows people down

Every physical task has a hard spot: little space, heat, a movement that forces you to turn your body, a tool that takes both hands. Write it down as it is.

These details help you think about whether the equipment can be worn comfortably and when a recording would need to pause. They also let you walk into the conversation with your team with concrete examples. What to agree with the people who would take part is in the guide to talking with your team.

Identify who knows the task

Write the name of the role, not the worker: the person who prepares orders, who adjusts the machine, who checks the packing. Also note who could explain it to a newcomer and who would coordinate the evaluation inside your company.

This part is easy to leave for later, and it is usually the one people miss most. A task described well by someone who does not do it can lose exactly the details that matter.

Note the limits of the place

Close the sheet with whatever constrains the work: schedules, areas where equipment is not allowed, safety rules, moving machinery, confidential information. If a company or client rule limits recording, write it down even if you think it does not apply.

Do not assume these limits will be sorted out later. It is easier to decide with them in view than to find out halfway through a trial.

A short example sheet

Picture a shop that assembles gift boxes. The sheet could say: it starts when the person picks up the empty box and ends when they seal it with tape. In the frame are hands, product, filler paper and the table; the order screen stays out, turned toward the wall. The product size varies. Leaning over the low table is uncomfortable. The morning-shift box assembler knows the task. Recording is avoided when customers are nearby. This is an invented example to show the format, not a task approved by Mirentor.

Use it to decide, not only to present

A completed sheet lets you compare tasks. The one you meant to propose may have too many sensitive areas, while a simpler one fits in half a page. It can also show that a task still depends on knowledge that sits in one person's head.

To go from the sheet to a limited trial, the guide how to prepare a pilot explains the proposed path: waitlist, introduction call, agreement and on-site coordination.

Bring the sheet to the conversation

Write one sheet for a single task this week, with the six points above, and underline what you cannot answer. That list of open questions is the best starting point for the first call, with no commitment to record anything.

What to agree on with your team before recording tasks at work

An agenda for the conversation before recording: why, voluntary participation, pay, the site, how to pause and where the video goes.

A camera can record a task while also capturing a screen, a document, or someone walking past. Wearing it while working can also be uncomfortable. Before arranging a recording, you need to discuss those situations with the people who would be involved.

Mirentor proposes collecting first-person video of manual tasks to help train robotics models. Voluntary participation and compensation for the people recording are part of the proposed program. Specific terms still need to be agreed; this guide helps you prepare for that conversation rather than replacing a contract or an approved policy.

As a business owner or operations lead, your aim is for the team to understand the proposal, have room to raise questions, and know the conditions before deciding. This agenda helps you identify what remains unresolved.

Explain why the work would be recorded

Start with the task and the intended use of the material. "We want to assess whether this activity can be recorded from your perspective to provide material for robotics research" is more precise than presenting the camera as an automatic improvement to the job.

Explain what you do not yet know, too. If someone asks whether these systems will affect jobs, do not promise there will be no consequences. A conversation about a recording trial cannot guarantee what will happen to employment in the future.

Avoid asking for a decision while the use of the video and participation terms remain unexplained. The team's questions are part of preparation, even when they require you to revisit the initial proposal.

Allow room for a voluntary decision

The proposal is for people who want to take part to do so, with agreed compensation. An owner's decision to explore the program does not replace each person's decision about wearing a camera.

Before starting, clarify how the proposal would be explained, how the decision would be documented, and what process would apply if someone does not want to participate or wants to leave. Also define a way to ask questions without having to resolve everything in front of the rest of the team.

Do not confuse leaving the program with the treatment of footage already recorded. What happens to that video, whether its deletion can be requested, and what limits might apply to that request are separate questions that need explicit answers in the applicable terms.

Put compensation into an understandable agreement

"There will be extra pay" leaves too many questions open. Participants need to know who would pay them, what the payment would cover, when it would be made, and how they could check the calculation.

Under Mirentor's proposal, the owner would need to define the share going to participating workers. This guide contains no approved rate or percentage. That allocation must be clarified before recording, without treating an indicative figure as an offer.

Separate the program's commercial terms from the worker's compensation terms. A commercial payment based on accepted footage does not, by itself, explain what a person will receive for participating or what will happen if a recording is rejected. Both parts of the agreement need a specific answer.

Ask for a written calculation example using figures approved for that case, distinguishing recorded time, accepted footage, and the corresponding payment. If acceptance criteria or a payment schedule are not yet available, the conversation is not finished. Do not estimate income by multiplying an entire shift by an indicative rate.

Walk through the site before choosing where to record

Review the task and what could enter the frame. Imagine, for example, a packing table next to a screen displaying customer data. Although the aim is to show how a box is packed, the screen could appear when the person turns. This is a hypothetical example, not a situation recorded by Mirentor.

Check screens, labels, documents, conversations, people outside the pilot, and areas that need to be excluded. Also identify whether the equipment interferes with tools, movement, personal protective equipment, or machinery traffic.

Do not assume these issues will be resolved later through editing or anonymization. Before recording, it must be clear what can be captured, what must remain out of frame, and what will happen if something is recorded by mistake. The site review should lead to specific instructions for participants.

Define how to stop a recording and handle an incident

A problem should not leave the worker improvising. If the camera is uncomfortable, information that should have been excluded appears, or a working condition changes, the team needs to know how to pause recording and whom to notify.

Agree on who can call for a pause, which situations require it, who reviews the incident, and what needs to happen before recording resumes. Check that people know how to apply those instructions using the equipment they would work with.

These measures must form part of a safety review appropriate to the site. This guide does not validate a device or establish that a task is safe to record.

Ask for specific answers about where the video goes

Before participating, the company and the team should be able to understand what the material will be used for, who may receive or access it, how long it will be retained, and whom to contact with a question.

If a protective measure is proposed, ask for an explanation of its scope and limits. Restricting access is not the same as removing identifying information, and no illustration on the website demonstrates that a data protection measure is applied to every video.

Write down unanswered questions and assign them to someone who can resolve them. The terms need a review appropriate to the location and intended use; this article does not guarantee legal compliance or absolute protection of information.

Finish with a clear record of agreements and open questions

At the end, summarize the task proposed for recording, who has expressed interest, how their compensation would be defined, what must be excluded, and how pauses and data would be handled. Separately identify anything that still lacks an approved answer.

If essential conditions are missing, the next step is to resolve them before arranging a recording.

How to prepare for a first-person work video pilot at your company

What to define before recording: the task, the first call, the scope, a limited trial and how footage is reviewed.

Your team knows details of the job that rarely make it into a written procedure: how to position an object that does not fit where expected, adjust a tool, or check that an order is properly packed. First-person video makes it possible to observe some of that work from the perspective of the person doing it.

Mirentor proposes recording manual tasks at businesses to provide material that can help train robotics models. Before considering recording at your site, you need to understand what would happen, who would need to take part, and which conditions would need to be settled.

This guide explains the proposed path for evaluating a pilot. The steps, commercial terms, and availability must be confirmed for each case; it is not an offer of participation or income.

Start with a specific task

Think of an activity your team regularly performs. Preparing an order, for example, might involve picking up a product, placing it in a box, and checking that it is protected. This is an illustration of the information a camera could capture, not a task already approved by Mirentor.

A recording from a worker's perspective can show their hands, the objects, and part of their surroundings. This material is also called egocentric video. It can provide information about how a physical task is performed, but it does not capture everything a robot needs to carry out that task.

For the first conversation, describe where the task starts and ends, which tools it requires, and what varies from one attempt to the next. Include the difficulties too: heat, limited space, moving machinery, or information that should not appear on camera. Those details help assess suitability without presenting your operation as something it is not.

Use the first call to assess suitability

The proposed path begins with a waitlist and an introductory call. That conversation would allow a review of your operation before discussing an agreement or arranging a visit.

You can prepare five pieces of information: your city, what your business does, your approximate team size, the tasks you want to evaluate, and any site restrictions. Employee numbers provide context; they do not, on their own, confirm that your company can participate.

It also helps to know who would coordinate the evaluation within your company. That person should understand the operation and be able to collect questions from the people performing the tasks. You do not need to have every answer ready, but you should distinguish what you know from what you would need to check.

Define the scope before arranging a visit

If there is a suitable fit, the proposed sequence includes an agreement and coordination of on-site setup. Before reaching that stage, ask for a specific description of the pilot: the tasks included, the people taking part, the equipment needed, and each party's responsibilities.

A task that sounds simple on a call may be uncomfortable to perform while wearing a camera. Preparation should therefore address how equipment use would be assessed, what interference would require recording to stop, and who would decide whether it could continue. Do not promise that recording will change nothing about day-to-day work.

The duration also needs to be defined, along with the conditions for ending or extending the trial. A pilot is not a basis for assuming continuous demand for recordings or automatic renewal.

Plan a limited trial

One practical way to evaluate a pilot is to start with a scope you can review: a defined task, people who have chosen to participate, and an agreed period. This is a preparation recommendation, not a trial size or duration established by Mirentor.

The company would need to coordinate site access and restrictions. Participants would need to know how to use the equipment and whom to contact with questions. Mirentor would need to explain the footage requirements, the review process, and the program's terms. The final allocation of responsibilities must be agreed before recording.

During the trial, document operational issues: equipment that makes a movement difficult, an area that needs to be excluded, or an instruction that is unclear. Do not use the number of hours as the only measure of whether the pilot is working.

Review the footage and the team's experience

The time a camera remains on does not automatically equal time accepted for payment. Under the proposed model, the calculation depends on material accepted according to the agreed criteria. Breaks or material that does not meet those criteria should not be counted as expected income.

Visible hands alone are not enough to assume a recording will be accepted. Before making an earnings estimate, you need to know how footage is reviewed, who confirms acceptance, and how payment is calculated. Rates and the allocation of payments must be confirmed for your case; this guide does not promise a financial return.

At the end of the trial, review two questions separately: which material was accepted, and how recording affected the work. A useful outcome should help you decide what to keep, what to change, and whether it makes sense to continue under newly agreed terms.

Bring a description of your operation to the conversation

Write down a task you want to evaluate, who performs it, what restrictions apply at the site, and what your team would need to know before participating. This gives you a concrete starting point without committing to recording.

For now, prepare a description of your operation and the questions you need answered before considering a pilot.
